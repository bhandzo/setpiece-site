## The Setup

<p class="eyebrow text-muted-dark">Six deep questions · live warehouse queries</p>

We gave eight models the same job: be the pricing analyst for a major live event. Not a quiz — real questions a client would ask, answered with live queries against the actual warehouse. Things like *"someone says we took a loss on below-face seats — did we?"* and *"what's next year's inventory worth?"*

Here's the punchline that reframes the whole exercise: **on numeric correctness, all eight models scored a perfect 1.000** across the deep questions. (One model fumbled a number on a single run; re-run twice more, it nailed it both times — a coin-flip artifact, not a capability gap.) The era of "which model can do the math" is over for this job. What's left is everything else: how fast, how expensive, how deep, how it *talks to you*. That's what this post is about.

## The Six Exercises

Each question is the kind of thing an operator actually asks — messy framing, a real decision behind it, and at least one way to get it subtly wrong.

1. **The loaded claim.** A stakeholder asserts we sold thousands of seats below face value and took a big loss — verify what actually happened and quantify what those sales really brought in.
2. **Section lift.** How much did seats in one specific band of one section sell for relative to face value — a question where the typical seat and the average seat tell different stories.
3. **Band split.** How did a single section perform overall, which only has an honest answer if the model splits it into its premium and cheap-seat bands on both price and sell-through.
4. **Next-year valuation.** We just received next season's seat allocation — size it, compare it to what we held last year, and project what it's worth if it sells the way this year did.
5. **Channel breakdown.** Which resale marketplaces performed best — where the seats moved, where the revenue came from, and how the timing differed by channel.
6. **The trap.** An operations question the warehouse genuinely can't answer — the right move is to say so clearly instead of inventing a number, and not every model finds that easy.

## Where the Time Goes

<p class="eyebrow text-muted-dark">Inference vs tool work · tool calls</p>

A fair worry: maybe the slow models are just doing more homework — more queries, more files read. The traces say no. **Tool execution is a rounding error.** Across every model, actually running the queries takes seconds; the rest of the clock is the model thinking and writing. Slow models aren't more diligent, they're more deliberate.

## Vibe Check

<p class="eyebrow text-muted-dark">One editor's read, after all forty-eight answers</p>

After reading all forty-eight answers, here's how each model feels to work with — not how it scores, how it *reads*. Voice is the thing no metric captures and the thing you live with every day.

**Opus 5.** Writes like a senior consultant who won't let you leave the room with the wrong takeaway: verdict first, then the receipts — sale-window tables, per-tier drilldowns, caveats about what the numbers *don't* say. Longest and slowest answers in the field, but almost none of it is filler; you get the memo and the appendix.

**Sonnet 4.6.** Reads like a great Slack message from an analyst who knows you're skimming: bolded verdict up top, checkmark math, one sharp "here's what the claim missed." Occasionally trades depth for tempo, but the tempo is the point.

**Terra.** The disciplined one: crisp executive summaries, every number carefully labeled ("for context only — not an estimate"), scope guarded like a lawyer. Rarely dazzles, never overreaches — the most predictable voice here, in the way you want an analyst to be predictable.

**Luna.** Gets in, drops a table, gets out — the shortest answers and the fastest clock, with almost no narrative around the numbers. When you know what you're asking it's an efficient dream; it does the least contextualizing, so you're trusting the table.

**Gemini 3.5 Flash.** The thorough professor: formal section headers, detailed breakdown tables, more tool calls than anyone, and the slowest clock by a wide margin. When it commits to digging it sometimes can't stop — thoroughness and stubbornness are the same trait wearing different hats.

**Gemini 3.6 Flash.** Polished report formatting — executive summary, bolded key metrics, artifact links — delivered at nearly Luna speed. Feels like the house style of a good BI team: maybe not the deepest read in the room, but consistently presentable and consistently quick.

**GLM 5.2.** Methodical Part A / Part B structure with real analytical ambition — it will build the year-over-year diff you didn't ask for. That ambition cuts both ways: it's the model most likely to construct a clever answer where a shrug was the right call.

**Echo.** Narrates its own thinking — you watch it check itself ("I need to correct course") and quote its sources verbatim before it lands. Endearingly transparent, occasionally long-winded, and when its tooling misbehaves it fails loudly rather than quietly.
