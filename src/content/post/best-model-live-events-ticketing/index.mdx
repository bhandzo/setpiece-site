## TL;DR;

If you’re building an agent to do data analysis for live events there are quite a few models, both closed and open weights, that will do a great job. Focusing on the "workhorse tier", we ran models through 6 quick question answers, and 6 deeper analysis requests to see how well they performed. We found that deciding which one was best for our workload came down to “vibes” more than any particularly metric.

We were able to start analyzing vibes by breaking down speeed, efficiency, and verbosity of the mnodels. Models like Claude who love tables, and words, had the longest responses, while the new OpenAI models respond like my Gen Z nieces over text.

We were super impressed with the efficiency of the newest OpenAI models, driven by their paralell tool calling, and very intrigued by Echo as a new entrant.

## Finding our pareto BFF

As part of our work on Extra Point I built a data analyst agent using [Mastra](https://mastra.ai/) to handle our day to day workload. This agent sits on top of our warehouse, and all the context about our business and clients that we’ve carefully organized. That means that we don’t need to use the beefiest model to get the best results.\
\
When trying to figure out which model was best for us, I didn’t find the public benchmarks to be particularly useful. I wanted to know what it would be like to live with this agent day in and day out, how it would perform in our harness, answering the kinds of ticketing questions we ask every day.

### How we did our analysis

I used Mastra’s [datasets](https://mastra.ai/docs/datasets/overview) and [experiments](https://mastra.ai/blog/mastra-experiments) to create a two tier benchmarking setup around our existing agent. This let us rotate models through the exact harness we use every day with the same tools and skills.

1. **The "regression tier"** is a set of "quick questions" and golden answers. This serves both as a model baseline and our frontline eval/test suite when making changes to prompts. Think "what was ATP for this section" or "what was the total revenue for this event". 
2. **The "deep analysis tier"**. I cleaned up questions we had actually asked our agent, and generated golden answers. I also built a rubric for each question which I’m still tuning.

### The math is mathing

Across both the regression and deep analysis tier, all the models we tested produced correct answers. I took some of that performance as an indication that our warehouse is in great shape, and some of that as an indication that we weren't testing for large open ended analysis skills that tend to produce more separation. 

Getting these kinds of results this widely shows how much the model world has flattened in the past year. When Sonnet launched a year ago it was in a class of it's own when it came to efficient agentic use. There are now a wide variety of equally good competitors.


### It's a vibes era

Given that all the models are capable of doing the basic analysis, I wanted to try to quantify the qualitiative parts of their answers. We built first versions of rubrics that do this, they're going to take some time to tune, so we started with simpler axes that are still quite useful. 

At this point most of us are familiar with model vibes. I for one am incapable of reading or hearing "load bearing" without thinking of Claude. As model ability converges, the "feel" of interacting with a model is going to start to matter more. Most power users already have some version of this they do for themselves, and now builders both internal and external will need to think about this as they build for their teams and customers.

Given that we were starting from the basics, we wanted to dig in a bit more on both what drove models time to respond, and whether there was variation in timing across cases.

The first thing we ruled out was the obvious explanation, that the slow models were just doing more work. They weren't. Every model made roughly the same 18-24 tool calls per question, and actually running those calls was a rounding error for everyone — about 5% of the clock. The other 95% 
is the model thinking and writing.

### Which middle school math quiz taker are you?

When I was a grade schooler, my KPI on quizzes was whether I could be the first to hand mine in. My least favorite request was to show my work. My wife on the other hand always needed an extra blue book for her written tests. 

Terra and Luna are more like me, blazing through paralell tool and short turns calls to try to get the answer as fast as possible, not providing a ton of color. Opus can parallelize but does it less, taking half the round trips, but thinking 4x longer on each one. 

There are two models of note when looking at things this way. First is Gemini, which only ever made a single tool call per round trip, but had by far the fastest token generation. Secondd is Echo which had some of the slowest tokens/sec but are early in their journey and I'd expect that to go up.

## What we thought about each model

Reading through the answers we got, these were our impressions. 

**Opus 5.** Writes like a senior consultant who won't let you leave the room with the wrong takeaway: headline, receipts — tables, drilldowns, caveats about what the numbers *don't* say. Longest and slowest answers in the field, but almost none of it is filler. Great for getting in the weeds, risks being ignored by team members with less time or interest.

**Sonnet 4.6.** Reads like a great Slack message from an analyst who knows you're skimming: bolded verdict up top, emojis, a sharp "point you might have missed". The classic "Claude" voice and tone, with its occasional drift into burying the insight inside the formatting. 

**Terra.** The disciplined one: crisp executive summaries, every number carefully labeled, scope guarded like a lawyer. Rarely dazzles, never overreaches, predicatbly direct.

**Luna.** The shortest answers and the fastest clock, with almost no narrative around the numbers. When you know what you're asking it's an efficient dream, but gives almost no cites, analysis, or illustration. 

**Gemini 3.6 Flash.** Polished report formatting — executive summary, bolded key metrics, artifact links — delivered at nearly Luna speed. Feels like the house style of a good BI team: maybe not the deepest read in the room, but consistently presentable and consistently quick.

**GLM 5.2.** Methodical Part A / Part B structure with real analytical ambition — it will build the year-over-year diff you didn't ask for. That ambition cuts both ways: it's the model most likely to construct a clever answer where a shrug was the right call.

**Echo.** Narrates its own thinking — you watch it check itself ("I need to correct course") and quote its sources verbatim before it lands. Endearingly transparent, occasionally long-winded, and when its tooling misbehaves it fails loudly rather than quietly, clearly a work in progress. 

# Where did we land?

We're trying an experiment using Mastra's request context to route simple questions to Luna and complex questions to Opus. We will report back on whether this felt like a good optimization, or if it made us nervous about which version of the analyst we were going to get each time we asked.
